### Would the results change under independent re-annotation?

They might; the estimate does not speak to that case, so here is what it does. A triplet carries V votes, L left and R right, and its label is whichever side has more. We split those votes into two disjoint halves, take each half's majority, and ask whether the halves agree. Rather than resample we enumerate all C(V, ⌊V/2⌋) splits exactly via the hypergeometric PMF, so there is no Monte-Carlo error. Averaged over the 64,420 non-tie triplets this gives 70.1%: 100% on unanimous and strong majorities, 80% on 4-1, 50% on 3-1, 33% on 2-1.

A worked case makes this concrete, and Fig1_rebuttal shows one triplet per vote pattern. Take a 2-1 triplet, the most common pattern at 18,454. Three votes split into halves of one and two. In two of the three splits the lone vote is a majority vote, leaving the other half tied and broken at random: agreement 0.5. In the third it is the minority vote and the halves disagree. The average is 1/3; a 3-1 triplet gives 1/2 the same way. Those two patterns are 46% of the corpus, which drags the figure to 70.1%.

What the estimate establishes is the probability that a second draw of annotators from the same pool, at the same votes per triplet, reproduces the label a model is scored against. A model is measured against one such draw, so if a second would disagree three times in ten, accuracy above roughly 70% is measuring agreement with that particular sample of voters rather than the preference underneath. This is a property of the labels and not of any model, which is why we treat a 5-pp gap as uncertifiable rather than small.

The counts are low because the collection procedure solicited votes only until the first disagreement, so 47% of non-tie triplets carry three or four votes.

What it does not establish, which is the reviewer's question: it resamples inside the observed votes, so it sees only variability within one annotator pool. It cannot see a different pool, where demographic or expertise differences add heterogeneity; it holds wording and presentation order fixed; and a per-triplet model cannot represent learning, fatigue or anchoring.

All three omissions add variance, so 70.1% is an upper bound and a new group would be expected to reproduce the labels at or below it. Coming in below widens the gap between DreamSim's 80.7% and the floor. Reproduction well above 70.1% would overturn the paper, and that requires the observed counts to understate the true consensus, which is possible only if the early-stopping rule biased them downward. We will state the condition explicitly.

One line of evidence uses no bootstrap. Appendix C stratifies Cohen's κ between methods by agreement pattern, and it declines on weak-majority triplets exactly as the floor predicts.

The study we propose collects 3,000 triplets stratified across agreement patterns, over-sampling 2-1 and 3-1, with 30 annotators each and no early stopping against the ~35 needed for a stable majority at p = 0.6. A second condition has the same annotators judge the Section 4.6 manipulations, supplying the human data Reviewer o5ED asks for, and the 608 stimuli shared with NIGHTS-JND. The analysis is pre-registered against the hypergeometric prediction, with Table 1 re-scored.


### Fitting NIGHTS label noise versus learning a real but sparse signal

Both are occurring, and the three experiments below say in what proportion.

DreamSim's advantage over an untrained baseline breaks down as follows.


| Model | NIGHTS-100k | X-0 (published 20k) |
|---|---|---|
| DINOv1, frozen, plain cosine | 74.1 ± 0.4 | 88.6 |
| DreamSim ensemble, untrained | 74.5 ± 0.4 | 89.2 |
| DreamSim-Scratch, trained on 100k | 78.7 ± 0.7 | 94.1 |
| DreamSim-Pretrained, 20k weights | 80.7 ± 0.2 | 97.9 |

The ensemble is worth 0.4 pp over one frozen backbone, and training is worth 4.2 pp with no overlap. The published checkpoint adds 2.0 pp overall but 3.8 pp on the unanimous subset, which is the 20k it trained on. Advantage sitting on the training items is label fitting by definition, so 2.0 of the 6.2 pp is settled and the question concerns the other 4.2 pp.

The second experiment holds the representation fixed and varies only the head. Appendix D.1 sweeps ten similarity heads over one frozen DINOv3 CLS, so the only thing changing is the head's capacity to fit the labels.


| Head on frozen DINOv3 CLS | Params | NIGHTS-100k |
|---|---|---|
| Cosine | 0 | 72.01 ± 0.4 |
| L2 Euclidean | 0 | 71.56 ± 0.3 |
| Mahalanobis diag | 768 | 71.79 ± 0.2 |
| Mahalanobis rank-32 | 24,576 | 70.26 ± 0.4 |
| Bilinear rank-32 | 49,152 | 68.35 ± 0.3 |
| Siamese MLP | ~800,000 | 68.39 ± 0.5 |

Accuracy falls steadily as head capacity rises. An 800k-parameter MLP with access to both embeddings, their difference and their product loses 3.6 pp to a parameter-free angular distance. The representation cannot improve, so the extra capacity is fitting fold-specific label noise that does not transfer. It separates the two hypotheses without varying the representation. The five omitted heads are parameter-free and land within 0.7 pp of cosine.

The third experiment asks whether the residual is a low-level effect. Grayscale ablation cannot settle that, since color varies across the candidates here: they are Stable Diffusion samples from one prompt under different seeds, so removing color removes signal as well as any shortcut. We held it constant instead, binning the corpus by the chi-square gap between each candidate's RGB histogram distance to the reference, and measuring the trained-minus-frozen gain per bin. No image is modified.


| Trained − frozen | color-matched | 2nd | 3rd | most separated | 95% CI |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| DINOv2 LoRA cosine − DINOv2 ZS | +7.5 | +8.0 | +7.3 | +8.3 | ± 0.8 |
| DINOv2 Tversky/GCM − DINOv2 ZS | +6.0 | +6.8 | +6.2 | +7.3 | ± 0.9 |
| DreamSim-Pretrained − ens. ZS | +5.6 | +6.4 | +6.2 | +6.8 | ± 0.7 |
| DreamSim-Scratch − ens. ZS | +3.9 | +4.3 | +3.9 | +4.9 | ± 0.7 |

Each bin holds 16,105 triplets, and a color-only rule scores 53.1% on the leftmost and 67.4% on the rightmost, so the binning works. The gain is flat across all four: taking DreamSim-Scratch as the leak-free case, four fifths of what training buys survives where color cannot discriminate. A pixel-statistics-only classifier reaches 62.3% on the same folds.

Taking these together, the 2.0 pp of overlap is label fitting, and the capacity sweep shows that more fitting power at a fixed representation buys negative accuracy. Against that, the residual survives the color control, and DreamSim's confidence tracks the vote split in rank order better than any frozen model (0.760 against 0.617), inside the weak-majority regime as well. Part of it is sensitivity to human uncertainty. We cannot yet split that correlation into signal and leakage, because DreamSim-Scratch stored hard predictions only; re-inference is under way.

Section 5 gives what we think is the mechanism, and it bears on the reviewer's framing. Perceptual similarity is not one dimension: a voter must weigh pose, color, shape, texture, lighting, background and compositional identity, and NIGHTS asks them to collapse all of it into one bit. Where two candidates differ along a single dominant axis, voters weight it the same way and the triplet is unanimous. Where they differ along several axes at once, voters trade them off differently and the vote splits, which is what produces the 80,000 weak-majority and split-vote triplets.

On this reading the residual is not statistical noise but structured disagreement about which attribute should count, and a scalar label cannot record which one a voter used. A model fitted to those majorities fits one particular weighting, sampled from a population that does not share it. That is why the residual can be signal rather than noise and still not certify better human alignment: there is no single human answer for it to be closer to. The JND result follows from the same mechanism. On the 608 stimuli carrying both labels, all 2AFC-unanimous, the two tasks disagree 51.6% of the time and 64% at 10-0 consensus, because a different question selects a different attribute weighting. Fig3_rebuttal shows eight.

So we do not claim the improvement is illusory, only that NIGHTS lacks the resolution to tell. Separating the two needs per-rater labels, which is the first thing the protocol above collects.


### Generalization to other perceptual similarity datasets

The claim holds in aggregate and fails per category, and answering this made us find a reporting error. Both tables are in our reply to Reviewer fY3w (Table R5, Table R8). BAPPS is real photographs with five human votes per triplet, and per category the ranking inverts against us, since LPIPS beats every zero-shot DINO backbone on color, deblur and super-resolution. The abstract will say that plain cosine is not competitive for low-level distortion detection. Measured against each benchmark's own floor the claim survives: two methods clear both, DreamSim after training on a leaky checkpoint and a frozen DINOv1 with none. Section 4.5 says only DreamSim does, which is wrong.

We accept the presentation criticism as well. BAPPS appears nowhere in our abstract and has no main-body table despite being our only external validation, so it will be promoted.


### Less concrete guidance on a usable replacement protocol

Section 5 ends with a six-point specification, which we will lift into its own subsection. One similarity axis varies per triplet, controlled at construction time, and the 2AFC question names that axis rather than asking a generic "more similar". The release ships per-rater raw votes with anonymized identifiers and a recruitment summary. The vote count clears a target reproducibility floor, for which Section 4.3 gives the closed form, and fixed splits, tie-handling rules and a held-out distortion suite ship with the data.
